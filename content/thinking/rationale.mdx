# Rationale

A lot of my reasoning lives across the other three layers. Stated simply, the onboarding is a counterweight to overwhelm: one next step, never many, confidence and then connection, invitation rather than notification, AI named by action and never personified.

> A best guess, made better by listening.

## Why two layers

The thinking that is worth reiterating is that the two layers serve different purposes.

The deterministic layer is a best guess of what the leader needs, delivered on a predetermined, trigger-driven cadence. The conversation does something the guess cannot. It surfaces what leaders actually need and where the gaps are, across many conversations, and it gets closer to the real need before anything is offered.

Together: the deterministic layer moves, and the conversation shows whether the guess was right and quietly corrects it.

## The anti-dependency stance

This is an early, cautious choice. I think that the conversational tool should help the leader feel more competent and more surrendered, not more reliant on software. So it has a known, bounded scope made legible through named skills. At the edge of that scope its directive is to point outward, to a pastor or coordinator and to durable resources, rather than inward to itself. It should not do the formation or preparation work for the leader: no generated session plans, no materials that replace engaging the course. And it is never personified or branded as "an AI." This is an ethical position, but it is also congruent with the intent of the platform.

## What the training videos taught me

I watched several of the group leader training videos, honestly not all of them. A few things stood out. The training arcs deliberately, from an overview of the framework, toward *you do not need all the answers* and other helpful dispositions, toward handling difficulty in a group. That arc helped shape the week one to week three structure.

My own first reaction was mild overwhelm, partly from feeling I had to get through everything at once. I eased once I had the lay of the land, which validates designing for orientation first, then steps. My first instinct was to look for the Companion Guide, which might indicate that it should be surfaced clearly and early.

I also recognised a number of similarities between how Practicing the Way thinks about leading groups and how Project Exodus (my current employer) does: being a facilitator and a curator of the environment rather than the person with all the answers, and the power of listening and asking good questions.

## What I chose not to include

Leaving things out is the method here, not an afterthought. Reducing to the one next right thing means deliberately setting aside everything else, so what gets left out matters as much as what stays.

These are decisions of intent: no recruitment path, since an existing group is assumed. No countdown, which only manufactures pressure. No AI persona. No notification badges. No tool that builds the leader's session plans for them.

## How I would know it works

Movement on the signals the deterministic layer can see, such as sessions watched or the Companion Guide accessed, and conversations that leave the leader with one concrete next step or a delivered resource, all of which the system can record. A rising share of conversations that end by pointing the leader to real people would be evidence that the tool helps without creating dependency.

Beyond the signals, I would talk to people. I would sit with a handful of leaders who have been through the onboarding to hear what it was like in their own words. And I would design a bounded, selectively deployed skill that lets the conversational tool run a short, optional interview to learn where a leader got stuck. Just as importantly, I would deploy it to leaders who did move through, with strong signal in the system, to understand what is working and not only what is broken.
